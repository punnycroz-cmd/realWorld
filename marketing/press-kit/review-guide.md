# Review Guide — how to watch a spectator sim

Reviewing *Real World* isn't like reviewing a game you play. The default
relationship is watching; the paid layer is occasional and small. This
guide is a self-guided watch plan — what to look at, for how long, and
what would count as the thing working or failing. No presenter required.

If someone from the studio is running the preview for you, that's
`guided-tour.md` — this book is the same ground, walked alone.

---

## Pick your time budget

- **30 minutes** — enough for an informed first-impressions piece.
- **90 minutes** — enough for a real review of the spectator product.
- **One evening, unscripted** — the honest test. Leave it open on a
  second monitor while you do other things; that's how most people will
  actually use it.

## The 30-minute path

1. **Open cold (5 min).** Don't touch anything. Watch the block
   top-down. Residents leave for work, carry name tags, route around
   each other. If nothing moves, something's broken — tell us.
2. **Open the inspector (10 min).** Click a main character. Read their
   needs, mood, and recent thoughts. This is the whole interface of
   "watching a person": the inner state is the content. Follow them for
   one full errand.
3. **Read the public request feed (10 min).** Every paid request into
   the world is posted publicly — what was asked, what it cost, how it
   resolved. This feed is the drama log and the anti-grief
   accountability layer at once. Read a page of it like a serialized
   fiction feed, because that's what it is.
4. **Check the weather (5 min).** Look at the skyline. Distant shower
   cells render as columns with wind-leaned rain shafts — weather you
   can see coming, not a backdrop swap.

## The 90-minute additions

5. **Watch a request land.** If the feed is quiet, note that too —
   request volume is real and unpredicated. When one resolves, watch it
   resolve *in the world*, not just in the log.
6. **Follow a hired resident.** Ambient neighbors can be hired for
   bounded, disclosed jobs (the eight mains never — that's the ban).
   The inspector shows the difference: hiring terms are disclosed
   on-screen.
7. **Change altitudes.** Top-down for the neighborhood-as-organism
   read; street level for the Truman shot; director mode for the film-set
   framing with the REC cluster. Each is a different way of watching,
   and they're all free.
8. **Come back later.** Even 90 minutes is a slice. The world persists
   — leave and return, and the residents have kept living. That
   asymmetry is the product's spine.

## What "working" looks like

- You checked in twice and the neighborhood had moved on without you.
- A resident did something you didn't predict and the inspector's
  recent-thoughts gave you a legible reason afterward.
- The request feed read like a gossip column, not a transaction ledger.
- You stopped thinking about controlling anyone.

## What "failing" looks like — print it if you see it

- Dead air: long stretches where nobody does anything legible.
- Residents that read as pathing robots — routines with no inner state
  worth opening the inspector for.
- A request that seemed to reach into a main character's head. That
  would be the ban breaking, and it's the most important bug you could
  find.

## Traps to avoid in the write-up

- **Don't grade it as a game you win.** There's no win state for a
  watcher; the progression ladder (tenant→owner→landlord) belongs to
  player *characters*, and the admin landlord role is a system role,
  not playable. "You can't run the neighborhood" is accurate.
- **Don't call requests "control."** Requests are declared, priced,
  capped, and public — the world can absorb or deflect them within its
  rules. Possession doesn't exist.
- **Don't price-shop the credits.** Pricing is provisional; judge the
  *model* (agency by the minute, hard caps, public log), not the number.
- **Don't wait for voice acting.** There is none, deliberately — text
  and thought are the medium.
