Latest — devlog

Devlog · 2026-09-23 · Development build v26

Every request knocks before it enters.

A request in Real World is a suggestion to a world that can say no — and this week the "no" got teeth. The request pipeline now runs every filing through intent screening before it's priced or queued: one shared screening engine sits behind both the request form you see and the review console our moderators see, so the verdict you get and the verdict a reviewer gets can never drift apart.

What gets declined is a short, boring list, and it's published: requests that target a resident's private life, fish for secrets, try to steer a character who isn't yours, meddle in rent or evictions (admin-only powers), impersonate someone, or name a real business — the form will suggest the parody name instead. Declines refund in full, automatically, and on the public feed a declined request is one neutral line — request not approved. No spectacle, no wall of shame.

Gray-zone and exclusive requests go to a human reviewer with a target answer inside fifteen minutes, and every decision is appealable. What moderation never touches is the world itself: there is no "report the AI" button, no retcon tool, and the residents' secrets aren't in any schema a reviewer can open. You can try the filing end of this on the watch page's request simulator — same classes, same prices, clearly labeled as a simulation.

Sources: the screening engine and reviewer console ship in the world track's v8 tooling (world/screen.js, world/mod-console.html); the reason-code taxonomy and SLA are the machine-readable contract in world/moderation.json. The rules page summarizes the same policy in plain language: Rules & safety.

Earlier — devlog

Devlog · 2026-09-23 · Development build v26

How the block learned to look like the Mission.

The first pass at the neighborhood was a flat grid with good bones: real Mission streets around Dolores Park, generated addresses, 28 residents on schedule. Twenty-five art iterations later, the same block has pitched Victorian rooflines, water towers and solar arrays on the flat roofs, pigeons, a sun that actually tracks the hour — long shadows at golden hour, sodium lamps pooling on the sidewalks at dusk — interiors lit behind the storefront glass, and now the last streetscape details: parked cars on the curb, painted gutters, storm drains, and court decals out on Dolores Park.

Under the paint, the simulation grew its plumbing: a request pipeline that classifies every intervention (exclusive, compatible, queued), prices it upfront, and refunds what expires; and a lease ledger that gives all 28 residents real housing — deposits, rent runs, notices — because a world where nobody pays rent is a world where nothing is at stake.
