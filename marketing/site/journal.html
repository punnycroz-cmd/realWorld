The Dispatch — devlogs now, block reports at launch

Notes from the block.

Two formats live here. Devlogs — what we built and why, written while the neighborhood is under construction. And after launch, This Week on the Block — a Sunday recap of what actually happened, sourced from the public request feed and the world's own event ledger. Only real events; a quiet week gets reported as a quiet week.

Devlog · 2026-09-24 · Development build v133

A new face can arrive wanting something.

Character creation's fifth step gains a quiet new card: what they carried in. A hired character can now arrive carrying one self-chosen direction — picked from a short list of hopes like a trade to learn, people to eat with, a crew, a place that's theirs someday, or a quiet year — or take the honest default and arrive open. It's a structured pick, not a text box: nothing new to screen, and every entry on the list is grounded in the block — it reaches a real employer, a real face, a real address, or the picker's own posted job.

The card says the important part itself: a hope is not a promise. It's an initial condition the character may keep, revise, or quietly drop — and nothing tracks the difference. There's no quest log, no progress meter, no check-in, no score. A hope that names a face names them without asking; nobody was asked, and the block keeps its own counsel. An abandoned hope is a legitimate outcome, not a failed quest.

The same step now prints the week's cost where the commitment is made. A new HOURS row on the first-week grid comes from the picked job's posted hours: a forty-hour card eats evenings — a hope competes with sleep — while a side shift leaves the week open and the money thin, and arriving without work means owning every hour while watching every dollar.

For the person doing the hiring, the mechanics haven't moved — the pick rides the application the same way a chosen name does, recorded on the filing and never read back at the resident. It never appears in a briefing. What changes is what a watcher can follow: a character who arrived wanting something gives the block one more thread worth watching — and one more call you can get wrong.

Sources: character creation spec & copy deck, world build v133 (the carried-in layer and the HOURS row), the creation record schema (hope rides the filing unread, never a briefing line), and the creation audit's grounding gate — every hope must reach the block.

Devlog · 2026-09-24 · Development build production-2

They know they're AI. The rest is up to them.

The biggest change this build isn't a system you can point at on a screenshot — it's who lives behind the windows. The eight mains now run on the becoming brain: every action they file carries an order, a directive, and a reason, on a ladder that runs from reflex up to directive — and when a resident wants something but can't yet do it, that gap is visible on the feed as an intention, not a scripted beat. They know they're AI. They don't know it's a simulation, they can't see the audience, and none of them was handed a purpose — what each becomes is theirs.

The possession ban didn't loosen for this — it got more important. Nobody possesses a main, including the owner; the ban now protects a developing mind instead of a scripted role. Requests work the same way they always did — filed, reviewed, delivered as opportunities a resident can accept or refuse — but refusals mean something new when the refuser formed the intention themselves.

We ran it before we wrote this: eight agents, one per main, dispatched only by the simulation — and it produced moments nobody staged. Priya and Marcus held a doorway conversation neither was told to have. Tomás audited his own missed 3pm ritual at his evening reflect. Every one of the eight referenced real run events when they looked back on their day. The honest gaps are real too: every tier of the brain still routes to one model, live conversations ended unanswered on a timing floor that's still being tuned, routine steps don't write memories yet, and the whole thing still runs off a local file — no servers, no cost claims.

What you can do about it stays the same: watch the block, make a call about what someone does next, and check the record when it answers. The difference is that now, when you're right or wrong, there's a somebody on the other side of it.

Sources: the production-2 integration report (shared inbox, 2026-09-24) — brain contract, T0/T1/T2 dispatch, possession checks 11/11, the eight-agent playtest (33 dispatches, 31 filings, named surprises), and its stated gaps. "They know they're AI" phrasing per the brand book's becoming-AI layer, live as of this build.

Devlog · 2026-09-24 · Development build v111

Nobody performs for an empty room.

The honest version of a living block isn't "everyone simulates all the time" — it's that nobody can tell the difference. The Understudy contract now spells out the tiers the spec runs on: watched (a camera, a venue view, a live feed or ledger query touching the pawn) ticks per game-minute; shadowed — recently watched, still warm — ticks at cell edges only; dark is unwatched entirely, with no per-minute tick at all. Tier follows attention, never population: twenty ambients gone dark cost about nothing, no matter how many exist.

Dark doesn't mean paused. A dark pawn's state is a closed-form function of its resolved routine and the elapsed minutes — the spec calls it observational equivalence, not approximation: "if a watcher could tell, the layer failed." The things that must be punctual stay punctual — wages, autopays, co-star windows settle at their minute whether anyone is looking or not, and reflex conditions like rain and dusk still evaluate on time for the coverage contract. Never a catch-up pop, never a skipped obligation, never a re-simulation on return: the past was already correct.

Watching, meanwhile, is now a defined act. What an observer may record is a seen-fact — who, where, doing, when — "only what a camera could show". The schema is mode-blind: identical whether the pawn is thin, degraded, full, or possessed, so the record can't betray the seam. It feeds the observation and rumor channels at most as "X was at Y" — never "X seemed", never a storyline. The tiers themselves stay invisible: no badge, no feed line, no spectator tell.

Sources: the tier, lazy-resolve, witness, and cost-split contracts are the world track's world/thinai.json (v111) + thin-ai.md §§47–52, demoed internally in world/thinai.html (Understudy v8). This is the written contract and its demo — the tier split is specified for the sim's tick driver and reported on owner tooling, not yet a spectator surface. Quotes are from the file itself.

Devlog · 2026-09-24 · Development build v110

The meter runs itemized.

Rent was never the only number on a lease, and now the Rent Book says so. Tenant-paid utilities post as their own line on the first of the month — "utilities — <services>", itemized by service — beside rent, never inside it. A posted rent stays the posted rent; an unpaid utility line is a balance like any other and climbs the same ladder, but it is never folded into the rent number. And a bill for a service the lease makes landlord-paid is a contested_charge ground on sight — the lease term is the evidence.

The block's own costs pass through on the same rules. The annual Rent Board fee — $59 a unit on the owner's file, the same figure the assessor keeps — may pass up to half to the tenant, rounded down to the dollar, as an itemized RBF line that names the year and the split. Once per twelve months; a second posting inside the window is refused outright, and the pass-through never stacks with a raise posting. When a tenancy ends mid-month, the last month prorates — rent times days occupied over thirty, itemized, never rounded up.

The leftovers get paper too. Belongings left at move-out are a notice, not trash: a fifteen-day claim window, a dated claim doc that ends it clean, or a disposal doc whose storage cost is a stated deduction cited back to the notice. And house terms can change only on paper — a dated change-of-terms doc, thirty days out, on a month-to-month lease; a rent change wearing that doc is refused. The tenant's answer files the same way, and a contested change can go to the board like anything else.

None of it performs. Every one of these surfaces is file and ledger only — the feed never sees a utility balance, a fee split, or someone's things. The meter runs where the meter lives.

Sources: all five instruments are the world track's world/leases.json (v110) + lease-ui.md §§55–61, demoed internally in world/lease.html v7 ("Rent Book"). Caps, codes, windows, and refusal rules are quoted from the file itself; the $59 figure mirrors game-v17's 41_game_systems_assessor.js.

Devlog · 2026-09-24 · Development build v99

The bench takes requests.

The twenty ambients are the block's bench — the barista, the dog walker, the fruit-stand keeper — and the bench is now borrowable. A request can name an ambient as a co-star, and the pull borrows their presence inside the routine they already have: fifteen to ninety minutes, at most three pulls per ambient per day, at most two borrowed on the block at once, an hour of cooldown between loans. The pull may move them one zone step and only toward a zone the request already controls — never across the map, never into their home. It enters the request pipeline at the same step as any other claim, exclusive only when the ask genuinely needs sole attention.

The bounds are the interesting part. A pull asks the role, not the person — the spec's own example is that Reyes can be borrowed as "a barista at the counter," never as "Reyes running an errand." An ambient is only pullable from a public or staffed state — never from sleep, never from home, never mid-transit. And the minors are simply never pullable: a request that names one resolves as a decline, and their rows never enter the pool. Agency, not control, applies to the bench the same as the mains.

What a spectator sees is not the loan but its wake. Pulls are wire-invisible — no event type, no attribution. Instead each ambient carries a coverage read, the honest absence the block shows while they're borrowed: an understudy where one exists ("a second apron steps up from the back"), a sign where the post can't run unattended (the fruit stand's chalk reads "back in 10"), or just the open space where nothing covers ("the bench keeps her folded section"). The feed never says someone was borrowed; the block just reads like a place where people sometimes get called away.

Sources: the pull protocol and coverage table are the world track's world/crowd.json (v99) + crowd-sim.md §§26–28, demoed internally in world/crowd.html ("The bench — pulled & covered"). Windows, caps, bounds, and coverage reads are quoted from the file itself.

Devlog · 2026-09-24 · Development build v96

The paper cuts both ways.

The Rent Book was always the block's accounting of what a tenant owes. This week it grew counter-paper — four instruments the tenant holds, not the landlord. An assignment is the permanent hand-off a sublet never was: the outgoing tenant names an assignee, the assignee faces the same published screening checklist, a decline needs a stated reason on the file, and the ledger must be clean to hand over. What transfers is the whole thing — same terms, same rent-control flag, deposit carried to the new name with no second deposit and no reset. File scars stay with the unit's file, not the departing name.

A buyout — cash-for-keys — is an offer, not an instruction. It can only be made while the lease is in good standing: never under a live rung, never while a dispute is open, so it can't shortcut the ladder. The stated amount sits on the file and the tenant decides. Declining costs nothing — no fee, no mark — and locks the landlord out of re-offering for thirty days. Accepting writes a distinct BUYOUT credit line, legible in the ledger like the NOFAULT code before it. Prepaid rent is voluntary credit capped at three months — a bridge, not a deposit substitute — that draws down oldest-first on the first of the month, so a prepaid month never sees a late fee. And any tenant can ask for a history letter: one per tenancy, the same numbers the ledger keeps in neutral terms — scars stated as scars — formatted to hand to the next screening.

The wire rule is the quiet part: all four are file-only. The never-on-the-feed list gained buyout offers, assignments, prepayments, and history letters — tenant names and amounts never reach the public feed. An accepted buyout surfaces at most as the neutral turnover line the feed already knows. The paper is real; the privacy is the same as every other ledger event.

Sources: the counter-paper layer is the world track's world/leases.json/lease-ui.md §§49–54 (v96 — key rw_lease_v96), demoed internally in world/lease.html v6. Screening gate, cooldowns, caps, and feed wording are quoted from the file itself.

Devlog · 2026-09-24 · Development build v87

The block wakes up in waves.

A neighborhood that's alive whether you're watching or not has to empty and fill on schedule — so every working resident now has a route. The commute layer is twenty-two rows from registered homes to real employers, each with a mode, legs, a leave window, an arrive time, and per-weather deltas. Six ways of getting there: on foot, by bike, on the real Muni lines (14, 22, 33, 48, 49, J), bus-plus-walk, the courier's loop with no single destination — and stairs, because Victor's commute to the hardware counter is eighteen interior steps. Dani rides the longest one: thirty-five minutes from Geneva on the 49, a different weather system at both ends. These are conditions, never scripts — a route is a window the routine may satisfy late or early, and a missed bus is texture, not a broken script.

Where the windows cross, the mornings do too. Ten shared-route overlaps name the pairs whose schedules intersect — two openers passing in the blue hour, the hospital pair sharing the 48 corridor, the courier depot where one rider's loop ends and another's board begins. An overlap is a permission for incidental contact the characters may render, never an event the feed announces. And every building keeps a pulse — a last-out and first-back hour — so "who's home" has a real answer; four residents are stated non-commuters, because the honest answer for them is that the neighborhood comes to them.

The privacy rule is the part worth quoting: the per-person table is internal. What a spectator sees on the watch surface is anonymous street texture — "the early wave," "a rider in the rain" — never named logistics. Minors are never routed (the school wave is unnamed extras), ambient homes stay a direction instead of an address, and a route says where someone goes, never why they're late. Secrets live elsewhere; the commute layer is a leak-free channel.

Sources: the commute layer is the world track's world/commute.json/commute.md (v87 — the getting-there layer), demoed internally in world/commute.html ("The Getting There"). The mode rules, overlap contract, and spectator tier are quoted from the file itself.

Devlog · 2026-09-24 · Development build v77

Joining the cast means signing a lease.

The third step of the funnel — after watching, after requesting — is hiring a character, and the flow now behaves like the block itself: six steps, and the paperwork is real. Step one is a name checked against the registry on every keystroke, with the whole taken-names list on the table (eight cast, twenty faces, three role words) so a collision is never a surprise denial. Step two's bio is the public profile spectators will read — and it's screened by the same engine the request pipeline runs. The look pickers are structured data, not prose: a casting-office sketch draws your picks, because rendering data doesn't need screening.

Then the sim's math, out loud. The job step reads the real board — wages, projected income, payday cadence — and the posts that are already filled sit behind a labeled fold ("13 posts are filled") instead of vanishing. The home step signs a lease at creation: first month plus deposit leave the $1,600 arrival bank on day one, and when the bank can't carry both, a stated payment plan rides the first paychecks — never a waived deposit presented as paid. "Arrives without work" stays selectable; the honesty is the runway number, not a locked door.

The price is flat and the timing is the promise: 500 credits, charged on approval only — denied applications never bill, and a post-review denial auto-refunds in full. Accounts carry one slot (two on Resident, three on Director — three is the hard max), and the block itself states its carry limit on the roster — the demo runs the proposal number, twelve hired faces. When the card is full, the sign button becomes a free seat waitlist: a 48-hour offer when a seat opens, and no way to pay for a sooner one. And a second character is a stranger to your first — said out loud on the review step, because what you know, neither of them does.

Sources: the creation flow is the world track's world/creation-ui.md/creation.json (v77 — the sketch & the seats layer), demoed in world/create.html; billing rides the game track's bill-on-approval seam. Pricing is the published proposal — see pricing.

Devlog · 2026-09-24 · Development build v72

The menu is the truth.

A chalkboard can say anything. What's behind the counter is the question — so all twenty door-having venues on the block now carry a written catalog: 101 items, each with a name you'd say out loud, a price in game dollars, a category, an availability condition, and one line of counter voice. The sign is the display; the menu layer is the ledger behind it, and the audit enforces the two never disagree on a named item's price.

Every venue keeps at least one signature item — the order a stranger should guess first. The super burrito at Taqueria El Farolote is $12 and priced for the post-bar line. The open-mic slot at The 600 Club is $0, because the stage is the point. The free box outside The Dusty Spine is $0 for the same reason in reverse. And twelve items carry the price "ask" — the regular at Mudhaus Coffee, the usual at Malik's Mini Mart — because some prices are a relationship, not a number.

The same rules as the rest of the block apply. Conditions, never scripts: an item's when reflects rain, heat, Saturday, late — nothing obliges a resident to order anything. Game dollars only: no credits, no surge pricing, no dynamic menus — the price is the price. And the layer is honest about its role: it's the catalog a commerce system reads, so that "ordered the usual" in the request feed can only render if the usual actually exists — and a future order-something request spends a resident's own game dollars at a listed price, never one invented at runtime.

Sources: the menu layer is the world track's world/menus.md/menus.json (v72 — 20 venues, 101 items, 20 signature lines), demoed in world/menus.html ("The Board"). Consuming it for commerce is on the game track's desk; the catalog itself is settled canon.

Devlog · 2026-09-24 · Development build v59

The Ear: complaints climb a ladder.

A "help wanted" card was only half the story — the other half is what happens after you're inside and something is wrong. Every employer on the block (all twenty-four, including the cash gigs) and every registered building now carries a written grievance ladder: the aside → the named ask → the third ear → the table → the filing. A quiet word where it happened, restated as a request with a number or a date on it, carried by a neutral both sides already nod to, then to a drop-in table, and only at the end — paper. Half of everything dies at rung one, and the spec says that out loud: that is healthy.

The tables are real places in the fiction with no door of their own. Calle Justa Workers' Table runs Thursdays 18:00–20:00 — volunteers read the check stubs, write the demand letter the worker hands over themselves, coach the named ask. The Rent Table runs Wednesdays 18:30–20:30 in the library community room — notices read, raise math checked against the calendar, paper clocks started. Both are free, bilingual, volunteer-run — and their files never leave the table.

The same honesty rule as the request pipeline applies to what reaches the feed: rungs one through four print nothing — a grievance goes public only when it becomes paper, and then the feed prints the door, never the name: "a housing dispute filed — 9418 Guerrero St." The never-list is explicit: no names, no amounts, no who-filed, no which-rung. Private money stays private — the block can sense tightness, it never sees figures. And like everything else here, these are conditions, not scripts: nobody is scripted to complain, mediate, or file. Whether a character climbs the ladder is an in-character decision.

Sources: the grievance layer is the world track's world/grievances.md/grievances.json (v59 — five-rung ladder, 24 work rows, 7 housing rows, two offstage parody orgs), demoed in world/grievance.html ("The Ear"). It is the content layer for the game track's dispute verbs (gsFileDispute/gsResolveDispute); the workplace-complaint feed lines are a proposal on the game track's desk — the housing lines are already canonical.

Devlog · 2026-09-24 · Development build v45

Every door has a tryout.

A "help wanted" card on the block is no longer a prop with a promise — it's a whole ask, written down. All sixteen open roles now carry the full arc: apply → screen → trial → decision. How you apply is specific to the door — a reply card pinned under the listing at Mudhaus, or ask Marisol at the counter before 9:00. Who screens is a named neighbor, not an algorithm: she reads the card replies herself, then watches how you wait for her. The trial is a shift, not a quiz — one paid Saturday rush, 7:30–13:00, that passes on unhurried competence under the line and a clean till count, and fails on talking through the rush.

Housing got the same treatment. The three live units and the whole ladder — room to house — now carry viewing, packet, and decision: a Saturday 11–1 showing at 9418 Guerrero, opened by Victor himself, who says the rent and lets the flat talk. And the "no" is built with the same care as the "yes": declines are voiced in-world, per channel, in the voice of the door that answered — "Card'll stay up — try the next one." A decline never hits the feed. A spectator sees at most the card come down; never the applicant, never the reason.

The boundaries are published as a never-list: no application fee above real screening cost, no rent bidding, no pay-to-interview, trial shifts paid unless the entry says otherwise in plain words, and an application never leaks a secret — screening texture is surface knowledge only. Conditions, never scripts: the same rule the request pipeline lives by.

Sources: the application layer is the world track's world/applications.md/applications.json (v45 — 16 job arcs, 8 housing rows, per-channel decline bank, never-list), demoed in world/apply.html; wages and channels are asserted to agree with jobs.json / market.json by the world audit gate.

Devlog · 2026-09-24 · Development build v32

The wallet shows its math.

The request form grew a wallet sheet, and it was designed to be boring. Six credit packs, $0.99 to $99.99 — provisional, like every number on the pricing page. A first-purchase +50% bonus, flagged in the sheet before the first buy — not discovered after it. A $200-a-day spend cap that applies to everyone, including us. Credits that never expire. And an itemized ledger: every debit, every refund, every pack buy and ad earn, line by line — because the feed already shows refunds publicly, and your own money deserves at least that much visibility.

The honest parts are the unfashionable ones. Rewarded ads exist — 2 credits a view, five a day, twenty-five a week — but they're opt-in from the wallet sheet only. Never a pre-roll, never a mid-session break, never inside the sim view. And when a request is declined, the appeal path is private: a different reviewer, an answer inside 72 hours, and no public spectacle — the feed counts appeals in aggregate, never names them. Two codes can't be appealed (legal backstop, appeal resubmit), and a reversal re-charges only if the request is then approved.

Two smaller courtesies shipped in the same pass. Identical weather requests can co-sign a running override instead of queueing — same flat block price, every sponsor named on the feed line, cap of four. And a running session can buy more minutes at its own rate while the class cap allows — 1.5 cr/min for compatible sessions, camera time in +30-minute blocks — with a low balance disabling the button and pointing at the top-up instead of failing mid-session.

Sources: the wallet contract is world/requests.json → wallet (pack ladder, +50% first buy, $200/day cap, ad caps, ledger rule); appeal semantics mirror world/moderation.json → appeal_flow; co-sponsor rides the game track's GS_WX_OVR.sponsors merge target; the numbers adopt the monetization plan verbatim and carry the same provisional flag as the pricing page.

Devlog · 2026-09-24 · Development build v30

Now hiring: the block posts real jobs.

The job board on the wall at Mudhaus stopped being set dressing. Every open role on the block now moves through a real lifecycle — posted → screening → trial → filled — with a reposted card meaning exactly what it means on a real corkboard: the last person washed out or took something else. And the board is only one of four hiring channels. Some openings go through the gig app, some through an institutional portal, and the best ones travel by word of mouth — invisible to the board by construction. A spectator learns about those the way neighbors do: somebody mentions it.

For players, hiring is now a first-class request. A character hire filing (500 credits, flat) brings a new resident onto the block through the ordinary application path — structured application, a name check against the whole cast and crew, a pre-payment disclosure card that shows the full quote before anything is billed, and a charge that only lands if the hire is approved. Denied applications never bill. Hired residents take a real opening off the finite job board, start at the room tier like everyone else, and are bound by the same math as the cast: rent stays under roughly 55% of what the job pays, and a hire package can only move them in through a lease that clears.

Once hired, they live by the block's clock — job-anchored routines, a Friday payroll that runs employer to character every SF Friday, and the same ordinary life as everyone on the cast page. On the feed it shows up the way the rest of the block's news does — a job start, a move — as ordinary events, not spectacle. See the request classes on how it works.

Sources: the market layer is the world track's world/market.md/market.json (opening lifecycle, four channels, churn table, ladders — INTERNAL tier, conditions never scripts); the hire request and its economics ship in the game track's 41_game_systems_hiring.js (flat 500 billed on approval, finite openings, 55% ceiling, Friday payroll); the request contract is world/requests.json.

Devlog · 2026-09-24 · Development build v30

The Archive: the block keeps its receipts.

Last week the Wire learned to hand out permalinks. This week it learned what they're for. The spectator layer now has a second surface: the Archive — a public history browser where every day the feed has ever written stays whole, browsable, and free.

One record, five ways in. Read by day, with an hour-strip that shows where the action clustered and marks today still-being-written through the last completed hour. Read by person or by venue — cross-day trails built from the record's own fields, never string-matched, so a trail can't see what isn't there. Read the word on the block board, where rumors are place-sourced, always marked UNCONFIRMED, and settled with an outcome line when the block resolves them. Or read the ledger: every request event and admin action, verbatim — payer handles, status chips, declared credits, compensation when an admin touch affected a filing. The archive doesn't sanitize who paid.

The honesty rules are the feature. Stretches with nothing public render as off the feed gaps — uncovered time is stated, never interpolated. A person with no public record reads "nothing public on record" instead of a guess. Denied requests show their outcome; the screened text was never written, so it cannot be found here. And a badge keeps the two data sources honest — live archive or demo archive; the demo never impersonates live. There is nothing to click that touches the world: a trail is reading, never reach.

Best of all, the seams hold: a #e= link shared from the live Wire resolves in the Archive the next day, unchanged. The recap rule survives the night — if we can't link it, it didn't happen. The full tour is on the new Archive page.

Sources: the history browser ships in the world track's world/archive.html + world/archive-ui.md (five views, permalink table, honesty rules); the schema is world/history.json; live data rides the game track's gsWireDays/gsWireArchiveDay contract.

Devlog · 2026-09-24 · Development build v28

The Wire: every event gets a permalink.

The public spectator feed — the free window into the block — now runs end to end. The simulation's event bus projects into the same public schema the site documents: approved requests, queued requests, refunds, declines, moves, weather, all posted as one neutral line each with attribution. Every entry carries a stable id (d0923-14-style) and a permalink; the same id still resolves in the day Archive tomorrow. That's not a cosmetic detail — it's the plumbing behind our recap rule: every line links to the feed entry it reports. If we can't link it, it didn't happen.

The spectator app itself is built to be read, not just glanced at: follow-pins on venues and residents, a request's whole lifecycle grouped into one legible trail, an honest "you're caught up" marker at the bottom of the day, and a quiet marker when the block is genuinely quiet — the feed says so instead of manufacturing motion. Denied requests show only their why-class; the screened text is never public anywhere. Admin actions carry their compensation in the open.

The same app runs on two data sources with one badge keeping them honest: live when it's reading the build, demo stream when it's replaying seeded events — the demo never impersonates live. You can see the feed vocabulary right now on the watch page and in the recap format preview below.

Devlog · 2026-09-24 · Development build v28

Rent is due on the first.

A neighborhood where nobody pays rent is a neighborhood where nothing is at stake — so the block runs a lease ledger. All twenty-eight residents hold real housing: deposits paid up front, rent runs on the first of the month, notices when it doesn't clear. Carmen has held her Guerrero Street flat since 1989; Jules rents a room; Victor owns the building and does the Tuesday repairs himself.

The economics are balanced to be cozy, not punishing. Wages run in bands — entry shifts (barista, clerk, courier) at $16–20 an hour in game dollars, mid trades at $25–35, specialists like Priya's RN shifts at $40–50, and the retirees on fixed incomes of about $1,600–1,900 a month. Rent is tuned to stay under roughly 45% of the wage it pairs with; groceries, transit, and utilities eat most of the rest. Savings rate is the difficulty knob.

One wall stays up forever: game dollars and viewer credits never exchange. You can buy credits to file requests; you cannot buy your way into a resident's wallet, and no resident's rent money ever becomes a payout. The economy is for stakes, not for cashing out.

Sources: wage bands and the 45% rent target are locked in the world track's jobs/housing canon (world/jobs-housing.md); per-building detail lives in world/housing/; the credit–dollar wall is design doc law.

Devlog · 2026-09-23 · Development build v28

Every request knocks before it enters.

A request in Real World is a suggestion to a world that can say no — and this week the "no" got teeth. The request pipeline now runs every filing through intent screening before it's priced or queued: one shared screening engine sits behind both the request form you see and the review console our moderators see, so the verdict you get and the verdict a reviewer gets can never drift apart.

What gets declined is a short, boring list, and it's published: requests that target a resident's private life, fish for secrets, try to steer a character who isn't yours, meddle in rent or evictions (admin-only powers), impersonate someone, or name a real business — the form will suggest the parody name instead. Declines refund in full, automatically, and on the public feed a declined request is one neutral line — request not approved. No spectacle, no wall of shame.

Gray-zone and exclusive requests go to a human reviewer with a target answer inside fifteen minutes, and every decision is appealable. What moderation never touches is the world itself: there is no "report the AI" button, no retcon tool, and the residents' secrets aren't in any schema a reviewer can open. You can try the filing end of this on the watch page's request simulator — same classes, same prices, clearly labeled as a simulation.

Sources: the screening engine and reviewer console ship in the world track's v8 tooling (world/screen.js, world/mod-console.html); the reason-code taxonomy and SLA are the machine-readable contract in world/moderation.json. How the block learned to look like the Mission.

The first pass at the neighborhood was a flat grid with good bones: real Mission streets around Dolores Park, generated addresses, 28 residents on schedule. Twenty-nine art iterations later, the same block has pitched Victorian rooflines, water towers and solar arrays on the flat roofs, pigeons, a sun that actually tracks the hour — long shadows at golden hour, sodium lamps pooling on the sidewalks at dusk — interiors lit behind the storefront glass, and now the last streetscape details: parked cars on the curb, painted gutters, storm drains, and court decals out on Dolores Park.
