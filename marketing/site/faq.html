FAQ

Straight answers.

The design is locked; the game is in active development. Everything below describes the designed product — where something is still being built, we say so.

The basics

What is Real World?

A persistent, browser-based life simulation — a "Truman Show" you can visit. It's set on real streets around Dolores Park in San Francisco's Mission District, where 28 fictional residents live around the clock on AI. The eight mains are AIs that know they're AI — born with no assigned purpose, unaware it's a simulation. Watching is free. Players pay only for agency: time-boxed requests inside the world, or a character of their own. "The Mission" is the first neighborhood.

Do the residents know they're AI?

The eight mains do — they know what they are, and nobody assigned them a purpose, so each is deciding what it's for. What they don't know is that it's a simulation or that anyone is watching. The twenty ambient neighbors run on schedules and reflexes, not full minds.

Is it free?

Watching is free, always — that's the core of the product, not a trial. Paid agency works through requests (priced upfront in credits, capped hard) and character creation. You can also earn small amounts of credits by watching opt-in ads, capped per day.

When can I play?

The game is in active development; there is no public build yet. This site describes the locked design. When a launch date exists, it will be announced here first.

What platform?

The browser — desktop-first. No install, no app store. The spectator experience (the neighborhood and the public feed) is designed to travel well to smaller screens.

How is it different from The Sims, InZOI, or Paralives?

Different verb. Traditional life sims are about control — you build, dress, and steer your people. Real World is about witness and influence: the eight main characters have their own AI minds, keep their own secrets, and can never be possessed by anyone. You watch the neighborhood live, file bounded requests the residents can accept or refuse, and — if you want in — create a character of your own who rents an apartment and joins the cast. Watching costs nothing, forever.

Is the neighborhood a real place?

The streets and park are real Mission District geography — real street names, real corners. Every resident and every house number is fictional: generated addresses sit beyond the real blocks' number ranges, so no fictional door ever maps to a real household.

Is it multiplayer?

One neighborhood, shared by everyone — there are no instanced copies. Every viewer and every player watches the same block, and every request lands on the same public feed. Compatible requests can even share a scene: two players steering two different hired characters through the same afternoon is allowed, and it's the best version of multiplayer here. What it isn't: co-op control of the cast — nobody steers anyone else's story.

Can I watch events I missed?

Yes — that's what the Archive is for. The history browser replays the public record by day, by person, and by venue, plus the rumor board and the request ledger — free, with a permalink on every record. Today's archive holds through the last completed hour, and uncovered time shows as honest off the feed gaps rather than filler. (On this site it runs as a demo until the game ships.)

Is there a day/night cycle?

The block keeps real time. A real solar engine drives the light — sun position, shadows, and streetlights all follow the actual hour — and residents keep routines around it: the coffee shop opens at 6 a.m., the park empties at night. Log off and nothing pauses.

The cast & possession

Can I possess the main characters?

No — never. The eight mains can't be possessed by anyone: not players, not the game's owner. Their choices stay their own and their secrets stay theirs. This is the rule that makes watching mean something — we call it the Truman Show contract.

So who can I control?

Only a character you create and hire yourself — your own tenant of the neighborhood. Not other players' characters, not ambient neighbors, not any of the eight mains.

Does possessing my character reveal its secrets?

No. Your possession briefing contains a public profile, surface relationships, and a daily routine. Drama seeds — yours included — stay redacted. You discover secrets the same way viewers do: by watching.

What happens to my character when I log off?

It drops to lightweight AI — a quiet, cheap version of itself that keeps its routine — and wakes to a full mind when you return. The neighborhood doesn't pause for anyone.

Requests, credits & money

How does a request work?

You declare an action and a duration upfront — "possess my character for 30 minutes," "rain for two hours" — and pay credits scaled to that duration. The system classifies it as exclusive, compatible, or queued; enforces first-come-first-served ordering with cooldowns; and caps the charge hard. When time ends, control returns gracefully to the AI.

Can I co-sign someone else's request?

Yes — compatible requests accept co-sponsors: multiple players can attach to the same request, and every sponsor is named on the public feed record. It's how the block funds shared moments — a venue's big night, a park event, a rain delay everyone secretly wanted. Exclusive requests stay single-owner by definition. (The wire demo on this site shows sponsored events with their co-sponsors listed.)

Can I link to a specific moment or request?

Yes. Every public record carries a stable permalink — events as #e=<id>, requests as #r=<id> — and the Archive resolves them against the full history. Linking a moment is linking a receipt: the permalink shows the whole status trail, so a request that was denied or refunded says so on its own record.

What if my request never activates?

Queued requests that expire before activation are automatically refunded. If an admin action overrides something mid-run, affected players are compensated in credits. Everything lands on the public feed, so refunds are visible to all.

Is there a subscription?

Two optional tiers are planned: Resident ($4.99/mo — monthly credit stipend, an extra character slot, queued-request tie-breaks, weekly life digest) and Director ($11.99/mo — larger stipend, camera-director spectator tools, cosmetics, name in the show credits). Perks are discounted currency, convenience, and cosmetics — never power over free players. Watching stays free without either.

Is there surge pricing?

Yes, and it's honest about it: a resource that was exclusively used in the last few hours, or prime-time windows, can carry a multiplier — always displayed on the request form before you pay, never added after. Cooldowns themselves are never for sale.

Do credits expire?

No — no expiration, no dormancy fees. Queued requests that expire before activation auto-refund, and admin overrides that cancel a running session compensate in credits.

Can I earn credits without paying?

Yes — opt-in rewarded ads, capped per day. Enough for small compatible requests with patience. Credits can't be earned by grinding; that's a deliberate wall against farming and gray markets.

Can I cash out, trade, or sell credits?

No — ever. Credits are non-transferable and non-redeemable, and there's no exchange between credits and in-game dollars in either direction. This isn't a missing feature; it's the wall that keeps the economy a game instead of a money service.

Are there loot boxes or gacha?

No. Everything purchasable is a direct purchase with a printed price — credit packs, a subscription, request time, character slots, cosmetics. Nothing is randomized.

What's the difference between credits and game dollars?

Credits are the meta currency (agency: requests, creation). Game dollars are the in-world currency (rent, wages, groceries) and are earned only by living in the world. Your character pays rent and holds a job like every other resident — nobody buys their way out of the sim.

Can my character get a job — and who pays them?

Yes. The neighborhood runs a real job board with finite openings at the parody venues — your character applies through the same structured path as every resident, and employers pay wages in game dollars on the Friday payroll. Jobs aren't decoration: rent-to-income is capped, so steady work is how a character affords a better place. Bringing your own character into the world costs a one-time character-slot fee that's billed only if the application is approved — denied applications never charge.

Can my character be evicted?

Yes. Miss rent and the admin landlord can evict — through admin tools, with a public record. New characters get no exemptions. That's what makes owning a place mean something.

Conduct & moderation

Can players grief the neighborhood?

Structurally discouraged: requests can't target harm, humiliation, or property destruction; cooldowns and surge pricing sit on world-scale actions; abusive requests are denied before they run; and the public feed makes every intervention attributable. Reputation consequences persist on characters too — the block remembers.

Why don't characters have voices?

Deliberate scope. The cast thinks and speaks in text — dramatically cheaper to run at 24/7 scale, and every word stays loggable for moderation. Voice is not on the launch plan.

Who runs the world?

The game's owner is the neighborhood's sole landlord and admin, acting through admin tools — never by possessing anyone. One of the eight mains is the landlord character inside the fiction. Every admin action is written to the public request feed, so the audience sees exactly what the management does.

Is this an NFT/crypto thing?

No. There's no token, no chain, no speculative asset — credits are a closed, non-transferable in-game currency, and nothing in the world is redeemable for real money.

Who moderates the requests?

Screening is automatic on every request's text — intent only, never a prediction of what the AI will do. Compatible requests run on classifier pass; exclusive and gray-zone requests wait for a human reviewer. We don't promise review times — a request that expires before activation auto-refunds. What moderators can't do is just as defined: nobody edits or deletes an event that already ran, and nobody scripts the residents. The full policy in plain language is on the rules page.
