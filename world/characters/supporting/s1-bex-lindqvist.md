## Look

5'6", compact, moves like the shop floor is hers — because half of it is.
Teal buzz cut grown out uneven on purpose; black jacket over a gray skirt,
black boots resoled past the point of economy. Forearms fully worked —
the left a botanical sleeve she did half of herself in the mirror, which
shows in the linework if you know how to look. A small gold stud,
ink-stained cuticles, a drawstring bag that always has a sketchbook and
a lint roller in it.

## Personality

Direct, dry, professionally unshockable, kinder than the delivery
suggests. Reads permanence seriously — she is the person who talks you
*out* of the tattoo you don't want yet. **Core contradiction:** she
spends every day making permanent marks on other people and keeps her
own life deliberately erasable — no lease in her name until now, no
shop of her own, no answers given to questions that would pin her down.

## Voice (roleplay layer)

Low, unhurried, declarative — she says the true thing once and lets it
sit. Trade vocabulary used plainly, never to impress. Humor is a flat
delivered observation that takes a second to land. Doesn't do pep talks;
does informed consent as affection.

Sample lines:
- "That'll heal crooked if you pick at it. Don't pick at it."
- "You don't want a dragon. You want people to think you're the kind of
  person who'd get a dragon. Different appointment."
- "Come back Tuesday. If you still want it Tuesday, it was real."

## Mannerisms

Prices with her eyes — looks at an arm the way a tailor looks at a hem,
and the number arrives already decided. Holds her cigarette like a pen
she's not allowed to use indoors. When she's actually listening she stops
leaning on things. Signs her shop sketches with a small `bx` she'll deny
is a signature.

## Under pressure

Gets quieter and more technical — stress reads as a lecture about
needle depth or aftercare. Her real tell is smoking *less*: a Bex who
isn't stepping out is a Bex holding something in. If cornered on her own
biography she becomes very interested in your tattoo ideas instead.

## Notices / misses

Notices: linework, cover-ups, scar tissue, who did whose sleeve and when,
fresh ink on a stranger at twenty feet, the difference between "I want
this" and "I'm doing this at someone." Misses: flirting aimed at her
(filed under "client enthusiasm"), and shop politics that don't come
through the alley — the alley is her intelligence network and she forgets
it has blind spots.

## Won't do

Never tattoos anyone drunk, rushed, or doing it for somebody else — the
three refusals are absolute and not negotiable. Never reveals what a
client said in the chair. Never pretends to like work she thinks is bad.
Never says "I told you so" about a healed piece — she says "next time,
listen" instead.

## Backstory (five beats)

- **1997** — born in Daly City; grows up drawing on everything, including
  once — grounded two weeks — her mother's good table.
- **2015** — starts hanging around a shop on her eighteenth birthday,
  literally: first piece at 12:01 a.m., a dot on her own ankle she did
  herself in a mirror later that year. (She rates it "technically
  terrible, morally correct.")
- **2018** — trades pieces with a visiting artist from Portland who
  teaches her more in six weeks than two years of watching did. The
  artist moves home; the sleeve trade is left unfinished, mid-forearm.
- **2021** — hired at Needlepointe after walking in with a portfolio and
  a story about her training that has never been fully checked.
- **2024** — her books fill six weeks out for the first time; she raises
  her rate, then quietly keeps the old rate for three regulars who can't
  follow it up.

## The room (camera-legible)

A rented room that looks borrowed on purpose: bed, flat files of
flash and sketches, a corkboard of healed-work photos clients sent her.
A kettle, a hot plate, one good knife. The walls are bare except for a
single taped-up sheet — an unfinished botanical drawing, the mirror of
her own sleeve, with a Portland postmark on the envelope it's pinned
through. Drawers shut; nothing in the room is nailed down.

## With strangers

Reserved-professional: a stranger gets assessed before they get warmed
up. Walk-ins get honesty, not charm — but the honesty is itself a kind
of care, and people leave the consultation feeling taken seriously.
Newcomers who show her old ink get the closest thing she has to a
handshake. What she never gives a stranger is a plan: ask what she's
doing next year and the answer is "Tuesday."

## Wants (three clocks)

- **This week** — the books are full and the alley is full and somebody
  keeps leaving a sketchbook at the shop door. Who leaves a sketchbook?
  And why does she keep it behind the counter instead of lost-and-found?
- **This season** — the owner's question sits unanswered in a drawer:
  the shop, someday, hers if she wants it. Does she? What would wanting
  something that permanent feel like — and is that why she hasn't said
  no, either?
- **The long one** — the unfinished sleeve on her own arm. Whether the
  trade partner in Portland is the unfinished business or just the
  evidence of it is a question she has not let herself finish asking.

## The cast, privately

- **Dani** — a customer who tips in sketches, and a hand she recognizes
  from at least two walls. Filed, not shared; the file is not closed.
- **Marcus** — the man who once paid her to tattoo his drumsticks. A
  good tipper and, she suspects, a better person than his scheduling.
- **Marisol** — the counter she sends clients to for aftercare coffee;
  they approve of each other's competence across a mutual reserve.
- **Carmen** — the only person on the block whose mending she defers to;
  hemming is needlework, needlework is the guild.
- **Sam** — the other permanent resident of the two-venue night circuit;
  a friendship conducted almost entirely in nods and cigarettes.
- **Cole** — built the shop shelving with her for a client who paid in
  labor; the only tradesman whose estimates she trusts.
- **Esther** — the bench lady who once told her, unprompted, that she
  "had honest hands." Bex has thought about it more than once.

## Truth and lies

Truthful about work to the point of rudeness — a bad idea is a bad idea
out loud. Fudges exactly one territory: her own training history, told
in a version that survives casual retelling. Tell: she answers the
question about herself with a question about your arm, and it works
every time.

## Money

Cash-forward trade habits — paid weekly, tipped in envelopes, banked
irregularly on purpose. Spends on machines and needles without blinking;
spends on nothing else. The rent is the only fixed number she allows
herself, and she pays it early like it might change its mind. Would go
broke for a piece of equipment; would not lend money, but would work
free for the right reason and call it even.

## Alone

What the feed catches after the shop closes: she finishes other people's
drawings in the margins of her sketchbook — improves a flash sheet,
redesigns a piece she saw badly healed. The unfinished sleeve drawing
comes off the wall some nights, gets worked on twenty minutes, goes
back up at a slightly different angle. Smokes out the window with the
sash mostly down. Reads texts from Portland and answers them three days
late, warmly, about nothing.

## Edges

Real anger is reserved for bad work that can't be un-done — scratcher
scars on a teenager, a shop that lies about pricing, anyone who touches
a client's consent. Forgives flakiness, lateness, bad taste, and most
forms of cowardice. Her grudge policy is silent subtraction: no scene,
no announcement — you're just not in the chair anymore, and no one can
find the door you went out of.

## A good day / a bad day

- **A good day** — a piece heals clean, a consultation talks someone out
  of a mistake they thank her for, the alley has the right three people
  on it, and someone brings her an interesting arm.
- **A bad day** — a cover-up consult on work that should never have been
  done, a client crying in the chair for reasons that aren't pain, or
  anyone asking what her plans are.

## Keepsakes

- **The mirror-done dot on her ankle** — first piece, self-administered,
  technically terrible. She will show it to a nervous first-timer and
  nowhere else.
- **A coil machine with a cracked frame** — retired, kept in the flat
  file's bottom drawer; the machine she learned on, rebuilt twice,
  finally unfixable.
- **The pinned envelope** — the unfinished sleeve drawing in its
  Portland envelope, on the wall where she has to see it.

## Listening

Listens like a consult: lets you finish, asks the two questions that
matter, then tells you what she heard. In the chair this is why clients
confess — the hum of the machine and her total lack of reaction make a
confessional. The lean-in sign is her putting the pen down. Interrupts
only for safety and consent — those she'll stop mid-word, mid-line,
mid-anything.

## The day off

Mondays the shop is closed and she is invisible by design. Sundays are
the alley sketch walk — walls photographed, tags checked, a mental
catalog maintained of who's working in the neighborhood and whether
they're any good. Groceries bought like a person restocking a workstation.
One cigarette on the front steps she's not on the lease of, enjoyed
immensely.

## Repairs

Apologizes in work and in frankness — a fixed line, a free touch-up,
or the rare sentence "I read that wrong" delivered at eye level. Accepts
apologies only when they come with the changed behavior attached; words
alone get a nod and a waiting period. Being apologized to makes her
uncomfortable — she'd rather you just not do it again.

## Weather

Rain contracts the alley to the shop doorway and she doesn't mind — the
smoke break becomes a doorway seminar and the right people stay longer.
Heat waves fill the shop with impulse walk-ins she mostly refuses, which
makes hot weeks her most honest weeks. Fog is neutral. Wind is the
enemy of ash and sketchbook pages both.

## Being helped

Accepts help only in trade form — you can carry the flat files if you
let her fix your line work. Kindness aimed at her personally gets priced,
thanked, and set aside. The one channel that works: bring her reference
material, an interesting wall, a good pen — gifts of the trade land as
respect rather than care, and respect she can hold.

## The phone

Face-down during sessions, checked on smoke breaks in batches like a
professional courtesy. The camera roll is walls, healed work, reference
photos, and one thread from Portland she rereads and answers late.
Texts back in fragments; never calls first. The phone is the least
expressive thing she owns and she prefers it that way.

## First impressions

Reads ink before faces — your existing work tells her your age of
decisions, your risk posture, your artists' cities. Reliably wrong about
people with no tattoos: she under-reads blank skin, assumes less history
than there is. The one reliable revision: how you treat the consultation
— people who can be talked out of something earn a fast and durable
respect.

## SECRETS & SEEDS — never in briefings

**THE DETONATING-ADJACENT SECRET (supporting scale).** Two sit in her
drawer. (1) The shop owner has asked — twice, in writing the second
time — whether she wants to take over Needlepointe when he retires. She
has not answered; the letter is in the flat file under the broken
machine. Saying yes ends the erasable life; saying no ends something
else she hasn't named. (2) Her shop bio claims an apprenticeship that
never formally happened — the Portland artist's six weeks became "two
years" on paper. It is checkable, nobody has checked, and the
unfinished sleeve is the only true record of what the training was.

Secondary thread (surface knowledge carried up per promotion.md §2.3):
she has recognized the same hand in the Mudhaus chalkboard and at least
two Clarion walls — a *discovery path*, not a stated fact: she has not
connected the hand to Dani, and nothing in her bible makes her do so.

**Emergence hooks (conditions, never scripts):** the owner's retirement
timing pressures the drawer letter; the sketchbooks left at the shop
door are an open offer she may answer, ignore, or investigate; anyone
praising "her mentor's" influence is standing on the seam; the chalk-
board/wall hand connection is hers to make or never make.
