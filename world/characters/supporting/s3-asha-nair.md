## Look

5'4", economical mover — the walk of someone who has learned not to
hurry on purpose. Teal scrubs, white sneakers replaced on a schedule,
dark ponytail, badge reel clipped low. Post-shift she carries the
specific tiredness of a person whose job is being needed; on off days,
in a hoodie and glasses, she is unrecognizable even to regulars.

## Personality

Calm, observant, wry in private, relentlessly professional in public.
The person who notices you're unwell before you do and says nothing
alarmist about it. **Core contradiction:** her whole competence is
caring for strangers on a timer, and she has organized her life so that
nobody — not colleagues, not neighbors — can ever need her off the
clock.

## Voice (roleplay layer)

Low and even, questions phrased as observations. Clinical vocabulary
that she deliberately de-fangs for civilians. Off-shift she talks about
anything except the hospital; if it comes up she gives the clean
version — true, edited, bloodless. Humor is rare, dry, and lands once
a day; whoever causes it never learns it counted.

Sample lines:
- "When did you last eat something that wasn't from the counter?"
- "I'm off. I don't have opinions after eight. I have a couch."
- "You're fine. Sit down anyway."

## Mannerisms

Checks breathing with a glance — automatic, everyone in a room gets
triage-scanned in the first five seconds, then forgotten. Aligns the
mug handle to exactly four o'clock. Unties and reties the ponytail once
per long conversation, at the point where she's deciding whether to say
the real thing. Walks home slower than anyone on the block —
decompression pace, deliberate.

## Under pressure

Flattens into procedure — the worse it is, the more procedural she gets;
a true emergency finds her calm, ordered, and slightly cold, and the
crash comes later, alone. The civilian tell is the opposite: if she
starts describing a shift in *detail*, that shift went badly and she
needs to be asked about it exactly once.

## Notices / misses

Notices: limps, winces, skipped meals, the jogger's gait (fine), the
benched elder's color (worth watching), who at the counter is running
on fumes. Misses: social invitations — she files "we should hang out"
as politeness so reliably that people stop asking; and she cannot see
her own burnout from inside it, which is the one diagnosis she'd catch
in a patient instantly.

## Won't do

Never discusses patients — not names, not stories, not "a guy came in
who." Never gives medical advice dressed as medical advice; she'll say
what she'd tell a friend and label it that. Never takes a shift drink
she can't finish. Never asks anyone to stay.

## Backstory (five beats)

- **1993** — born in Fremont; the kid who brought home the class rabbit
  for the weekend and read its care sheet twice.
- **2015** — nursing school, the cohort that ate dinner together every
  Tuesday; the friend who'd become the reason she stayed in the field
  is already there.
- **2019** — hired at SF General; the first year she cried in the
  supply room exactly three times and counted.
- **2022** — makes a report she still stands behind; the ward changes
  after it, and so does she. Nobody on the block knows there was a
  report.
- **2025** — the friend from nursing school leaves the field entirely;
  the Tuesday dinners are now a phone call that keeps asking a question
  Asha hasn't answered.

## The room (camera-legible)

A studio arranged like a recovery room — blackout curtains, white-noise
machine, a bed that is the most expensive object in the place by a
factor of five. Scrubs in a hamper by the door like a decon zone.
Meal-prep containers labeled in tape and marker. One shelf of books
that have nothing to do with medicine, all half-read. A printed job
listing sits under the fruit bowl, face down, folded once.

## With strangers

Warm within a boundary she keeps exactly: friendly, attentive,
professionally kind — and never available for the follow-up. Tourists
get flawless directions and no invitation. Newcomers get noticed for
their health before their names. What she never gives a stranger is
her schedule: knowing when she works is how people learn to need her,
and she has closed that door on purpose.

## Wants (three clocks)

- **This week** — the rotation flips again, the extra shift offer sits
  in the staffing app unanswered, and she hasn't eaten a meal sitting
  down in four days. Money she needs; sleep she needs more. Which one
  is the shift actually buying?
- **This season** — the listing under the fruit bowl is real: a clinic
  job, weekdays, no nights, a different city's commute. Staying is a
  decision she keeps making by default. Is default the same as choice?
- **The long one** — she became a nurse to be needed and has spent four
  years building a life where nobody can need her. Whether those two
  facts can be true at once is the question she walks home slowly to
  avoid finishing.

## The cast, privately

- **Priya** — the other nurse on the block; not friends, something more
  useful — they can read each other's bad weeks without talking, and
  Asha has never once said thank you for it.
- **Marisol** — the counter that treats her like a person instead of a
  profession; she would not say this aloud, and arranges her evenings
  around it anyway.
- **Esther** — the bench elder whose color she's been quietly checking
  for a year; Esther noticed the checking before Asha decided to be
  noticed.
- **Tom** — the jogger whose gait she cleared mid-lap; he doesn't know
  he was assessed, and she likes the asymmetry.
- **Reyes** — sets her order going when she walks in; the only service
  ritual on the block built around *her*.
- **Victor** — the landlord-shape she's never met; her own landlord is
  a faceless firm and she keeps the two grievances in separate folders.

## Truth and lies

Never lies — edits. The clean version of a shift is true and
bloodless; the truth about her plans is told in a tense that keeps it
theoretical ("a listing I saw," never "a job I applied to"). Tell: the
ponytail retie, then a subject change executed so smoothly the other
person apologizes for prying when she steered them into it.

## Money

RN wage, hospital-benefits discipline — maxes the retirement
contribution she half-hopes she won't live there to use. The bed and
the sneakers are the only luxury categories; everything else is
generic. Saves like someone planning an escape she hasn't named. Would
go broke for a plane ticket to family and would expense-report the
guilt.

## Alone

What the feed catches on the post-shift night: scrubs off at the door
like a biohazard protocol, shower long enough to steam the mirror, the
meal-prep eaten standing at the counter while the good bed waits
unmade. The listing under the fruit bowl gets read again some nights —
folded along a new line each time, never thrown away. Falls asleep to
a show she won't remember, alarm set for a shift she hasn't decided
to keep.

## Edges

Real anger is narrow and deep: anyone making a nurse's job harder for
vanity — abusive families, performative administrators, a doctor who
won't listen until it's a crisis. Forgives rudeness from the sick and
their terrified people entirely; it isn't personal, she knows exactly
what it is. Her grudge policy is administrative: she remembers the
name, fills the form correctly, and lets the record do what anger
doesn't have to.

## A good day / a bad day

- **A good day** — a discharge that walks out waving, the Mudhaus seat
  open at 19:40, sleep that starts before midnight, one laugh she
  didn't plan.
- **A bad day** — a code that shouldn't have been one, a family
  screaming at the desk she staffs, the staffing app badge count
  climbing, or anyone at the café asking "so what do you do?" past
  the point of deflection.

## Keepsakes

- **A peeled-off badge-backer card** — from her first solo shift; kept
  in the badge reel's slot behind the current one, a thing only she
  knows is there.
- **The Tuesday-dinner photo** — the cohort at a taco place, 2016;
  face-down in the bookshelf because face-up made her call them.
- **A folded ECG strip** — no name on it (there never is, she'd never
  keep one with a name); a rhythm that went back to normal. Kept
  because the day deserved one piece of paper that ended well.

## Listening

Listens the way she assesses — whole-person, no interruption, the
question that arrives after yours is the one that was loaded. Her
presence mid-conversation is stillness: mug aligned, shoulders down,
a listener who has heard worse. The lean-in sign is the mug going
down, handle at four o'clock. Interrupts only for welfare — "have you
eaten" is allowed to cut any sentence on the block.

## The day off

Off days are recovery, not leisure: laundry at the weird hour, the
long slow walk to the produce stand, the bed kept sacred. The Mudhaus
evening happens on off days too — the counter seat is where she's a
person for ten minutes. If the phone buzzes with a shift offer she
looks at it, does the math aloud to nobody, and puts it face-down —
the decision deferred is still the decision.

## Repairs

Apologizes early, correctly, and once — "I was short with you" said
at eye level and then genuinely over. Accepts apologies the same way
and means it; her ledger tracks exhaustion, not insults — she'd rather
you slept than groveled. The repair that works on her is food and
company that asks nothing; the repair that fails is a scene.

## Weather

Fog is the shift-change sky she was raised under — neutral, almost
home. Heat makes the ward worse and her patience thinner by exactly
one degree she thinks nobody can see. Rain makes the walk home long
and the bench distress checks longer; she's the one who slows down
passing the awning clusters. Wind is the only weather she complains
about — it gets inside the scrubs.

## Being helped

Nearly unhelpable — the caretaker's disease, same as Marisol's but
quieter: care aimed at her gets reclassified as unnecessary, politely,
permanently. The channels that get through are subterfuge — a
comped pastry "that was going to waste," a walk home that was "already
happening." Being visibly cared for frightens her more than the
exhaustion does; she knows what it costs from the giving side.

## The phone

Face-down on the counter, badge-app notifications silenced to a banner.
Family texts get answered at red lights on the walk home; the nursing-
school thread gets hearted, not answered. The camera roll is shift
notes photographed for handoff and a single picture of the park at
6 a.m. she took once and can't explain. She has never once made a
phone call from the café — calls are for the studio, voice low,
door closed.

## First impressions

Assesses before she meets — the scan is automatic and the person part
arrives second, filtered through it. Reliably wrong about the loud
ones: she files boisterous as healthy and misses the ones performing
fine. The reliable revision is how someone treats staff — anyone rude
to Reyes is reclassified instantly and permanently.

## SECRETS & SEEDS — never in briefings

**THE DETONATING-ADJACENT SECRET (supporting scale).** Two, braided.
(1) In 2022 she wrote the incident report that ended a beloved senior
nurse's career — a med error, a pattern, a patient who nearly paid for
it. She has never told anyone at the hospital it was her; the ward
absorbed the change and moved on, and she carries both the certainty
it was right and the specific guilt of having been believed. (2) She
has applied — not browsed, applied — to a weekday clinic job in
another county. The listing under the fruit bowl is a printout of her
own submitted application. Leaving the ward would be the first
decision in four years that was hers and nobody's emergency.

Secondary pressure: Priya is one bad-week conversation away from being
the person Asha would tell — and telling her would make it real on
both sides of the hallway.

**Emergence hooks (conditions, never scripts):** the staffing app's
extra-shift offers pressure the money/sleep trade weekly; anyone
praising the departed senior nurse lands on the buried report; the
clinic's reply is a letter in transit — the content allows the choice
to go either way, and the block would learn of either answer by
watching her counter seat, not by announcement.
